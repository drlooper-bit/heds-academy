The $47,000 Work Order That Sat for 21 Days: What Aged WIP Actually Costs

By Daniel Looper HEDS November 25, 2026 5-minute read

An open work order report sorted by age can hide a large invoice in plain sight. Here is an illustrative example, not a client result. A finished job worth $47,000 is still open 21 days after the last labor was posted, or longer. The work is done. The invoice has not gone out, and the cash is still sitting in work in process.

Daniel Looper, Founder of HEDS, says this from what he sees in the field, not as a published statistic. Most organizations carry work orders in WIP well over 30 days from last labor. Today most shops would be happy with 11 days. A 21-day ticket can look like progress against that habit. It still fails the HEDS 48-hour close standard.

That delay has a cost the month-end P&L does not show clearly. The gross profit on that work has been earned, yet it has not been counted. Labor Utilization compares hours invoiced on work orders with hours on work orders. The HEDS target is 100% to 110%. Hours that remain on an open ticket cannot move into the invoiced column. Location Absorption adds service gross profit to parts gross profit, then divides by the whole location's total operating expenses. The location target is 100% to 110%. Gross profit that has not been invoiced cannot help that result.

The close rule is not new. HEDS set it out in The 48-Hour Work Order Close Standard, published on October 26, 2026. Read that article at https://hedsconsulting.net/blog/work-order-48-hour-close-standard.html. Every work order closes within 48 hours after the last labor is posted. A 10 to 15 minute daily WIP review is how the rule holds on the floor. This article names the holds that keep a finished job open, and who closes it.

The three holds that keep a ticket open

Daniel Looper, Founder of HEDS, points to three holds that keep finished work from being invoiced. The technician has not completed the required notes, photos, labor time, and other admin tasks such as warranty requirements. Too much labor is already on the job, so service admin stalls while deciding what to do with the excess. The customer was quoted for the full repair, and there is now a dispute on the total.

Service writers finalize work orders and close them. That is the ownership rule Daniel uses on the floor. The writer still needs a complete file before the invoice can go out. Missing notes, photos, or labor time leave the ticket unfinished, and a skipped warranty requirement can stop the claim and the close together.

The second hold is a decision that no one has made. Excess labor is already on the job, and service admin delays the close while trying to figure out what comes next. The hours might be billed as written, or they might need a review with the technician and a documented adjustment that a manager approves. Until that call is made, the ticket stays open. Labor Utilization cannot move those hours into the invoiced column.

The earlier 48-hour article warned that delayed closing leads shops to write hours down, which pushes Labor Utilization toward the 85% red flag line. A decision on the morning after last labor is cleaner than a write-down 21 days later.

The third hold often begins with the quote and surfaces only after the work is done. The agreed price for the full repair is now in dispute. The dollar figure on the ticket no longer matches the figure the customer expected. The service writer can prepare the invoice, yet the close waits on a conversation that has not been finished. That conflict will not resolve itself inside an aging open-order report. It needs a named person, a call to the customer, and a decision on what will be billed.

Last Labor to Invoice counts work orders not invoiced within 48 hours of the last labor posted on the ticket. The HEDS target is a close inside 48 hours. There are no later aging buckets that make a 21-day ticket acceptable. Labor Recovery can slip as well if labor time was never posted in full. Labor Recovery is hours on work orders divided by paid payroll hours, and the HEDS target is 95% to 100%.

Use the daily review to clear the holds

The October article describes a 10 to 15 minute WIP review each morning. The service manager starts with the oldest open work order, puts one name next to it, and gets a firm close date. Keep that format. Use the same meeting to test the three holds on every ticket whose labor is already finished.

Confirm that notes, photos, labor time, and warranty admin are complete. If they are not, the technician has work to finish before the writer can close. When excess labor is on the job, decide that day what will be billed and what will be reviewed. When the customer disputes a quoted total, assign a name and a next contact before the meeting ends. The service writer then finalizes the work order and closes it. The first morning after last labor is when missing notes or excess hours should appear on the list. By the second morning the 48-hour line is already in view.

Start with the report you already run

Pull the open work order report and sort it by age. Look first at jobs whose last labor is more than 48 hours old. For each one, name the hold, then give the close to the service writer. For every new job, the close date should fall inside the 48-hour rule. Run the 10 to 15 minute review the next morning, and then every morning after that.
