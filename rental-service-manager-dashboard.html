Module 1 — Rental Service Mindset
Module 1: The Rental Service Mindset & Yard Flow
Goal

Master the core operational role of the Rental Service Manager as an internal fleet production leader: maximizing fleet readiness, eliminating turnaround friction, and enforcing strict stage-gate service level agreements (SLAs).

Floor Rule
In rental operations, the rental counter is your primary internal customer. Your product is a certified rent-ready machine on the green line earning revenue. Every day a unit sits dead in your yard or shop is cash burned.
Diagnose
  • Audit your branch's current Fleet Status Report: calculate how many total machines are owned vs. how many are actively on-rent or sitting on the green line ready for dispatch.
  • Review trailing 30 days of off-rent returns: identify units that sat in the yard for >24 hours before an initial check-in inspection or work order was created.
  • Ask the core operational diagnostic questions:
    1. "When a machine returns off-rent, what is the exact elapsed time before it is checked in, washed, serviced, and moved to the ready-line?"
    2. "Who physically owns the machine at each stage between the return gate and the green line?"
    3. "Does the rental counter ever turn away a customer reservation while a unit of that exact class sits dirty or uninspected in the back yard?"
Part 1: Fleet Production Management & Physical Availability
What It Measures

Measures how effectively shop capacity and yard workflow are directed toward converting off-rent, returned, or down equipment into certified rent-ready fleet inventory.

What It Does NOT Measure

Retail labor billing, technician billable hours, or customer-paid repairs.

Manager's Insight

The rental shop is an internal production factory. When an excavator sits in the shop for 5 extra days, the company loses $750/day in top-line rental revenue, while the fixed debt and depreciation on that $200,000 asset continue ticking. If the shop runs without daily throughput targets, it starves the rental counter of sellable inventory. Managing fleet production means tracking the flow of physical assets through each stage of readiness every single day.

Industry Standard Worked Example
FLEET PHYSICAL AVAILABILITY FORMULA
Fleet Physical Availability % = [Rent-Ready Units + On-Rent Units] / Total Fleet Units × 100

  2. Step 2: Count the number of units currently on-rent to customers (Status = On-Rent). [YOUR NUMBER HERE]
  3. Step 3: Count the number of units currently staged on the ready-line certified for delivery (Status = Rent-Ready). [YOUR NUMBER HERE]
  4. Step 4: Add on-rent units and rent-ready units together to find your Total Revenue-Producing Units.
  5. Step 5: Divide Total Revenue-Producing Units by Total Fleet Units.
  6. Step 6: Multiply by 100 to calculate your Fleet Physical Availability percentage.

Worked Example: Total Active Branch Fleet: 150 units. Units On-Rent: 105. Units on Ready-Line (Green-Tagged): 35. Calculation: (105 + 35) / 150 × 100 = 140/150 × 100 = 93.3% (PASS — Above 92% Target)

HEDS Target Benchmark:
  • Target Band: ≥ 92% Physical Availability
  • Warning Band: 88.0% to 91.9%
  • Critical Fail: < 88.0% (Signals severe shop gridlock or yard backlog)
Floor Rule
If your fleet availability drops below 92%, your shop throughput is choking branch revenue. The rental counter cannot rent machines that are trapped in your yard.
Part 2: The Turnaround Stage-Gate Pipeline & Internal SLAs

The elapsed time required to complete the four critical stages of the turnaround pipeline: Return → Check-In → Wash → Inspection/PM → Ready-Line Tagging.

What It Measures

The elapsed time required to complete the four critical stages of the turnaround pipeline: Return → Check-In → Wash → Inspection/PM → Ready-Line Tagging.

What It Does NOT Measure

Individual technician wrench speed or total fleet capital valuation.

Manager's Insight

Turnaround time is lost in the gaps between stages, not during actual wrench-time. A machine takes 45 minutes to wash and 1.5 hours to service, yet it takes 4 days to get back to the ready-line. Why? Because it sat in the return yard for 24 hours before check-in, sat in the wash queue for 36 hours, and sat in front of the bay for another 24 hours waiting on an oil filter. Setting strict stage-gate SLAs eliminates the dead dwell time.

Standard Operational SLAs:
  1. Gate 1 — Return Check-In & Photo Audit: ≤ 4 hours from physical return to yard.
  2. Gate 2 — Wash Rack & Decontamination: ≤ 8 hours from check-in completion.
  3. Gate 3 — Safety Inspection & Standard PM: ≤ 24 hours from wash rack exit.
  4. Gate 4 — Ready-Line Green Tagging & ERP Release: ≤ 12 hours from service completion.
  5. Total Standard Off-Rent Turnaround Cycle: ≤ 24 to 48 hours maximum elapsed time.
Part 3: Yard Dwell Time Taxonomy & Root-Cause Elimination

The 5 Root Causes of Yard Dwell Time:

  1. The Ghost Return: Delivery driver drops machine in the back lot without handing the return paperwork to the yard specialist or rental counter. Unit sits uninspected for 24–48 hours.
  2. The Wash Rack Chokepoint: Wash rack is treated as a catch-all queue with no staging priority. Quick-turn machines sit behind heavily caked dozers that take 4 hours to clean.
  3. The Unassigned Work Order Gap: Unit is washed and staged in the yard, but no work order is opened in the ERP. Technicians walk past it because it is not on their dispatch board.
  4. Missing Parts Hold: Unit is pulled into a service bay, disassembled, and discovered to need an air filter or hydraulic seal not in stock. Machine is pushed back into the yard with parts off.
  5. Ready-Line Tagging Delay: Repair and PM are completed, but the technician did not sign off the 4-C sheet or update the ERP status to "Rent-Ready." The rental counter still sees the unit as "Down."
Part 4: Color-Coded Physical Stage Tagging System

To eliminate confusion between the shop floor, yard personnel, and rental counter, enforce a 3-Tag Weatherproof Physical Hanging System:

RED TAG (Mechanically Down / In-Process)

Unit is not rentable. Down for mechanical repair, open PM, or waiting on parts. Required Data: Unit ID, Work Order #, Assigned Technician, Down Reason, Date Tagged.

YELLOW TAG (Checked In / In Pipeline)

Unit is returned off-rent; check-in photos completed; awaiting wash rack or PM lane. Required Data: Unit ID, Return Date/Time, Fuel Level, Hour Meter, Receiving Yard Specialist.

GREEN TAG (Certified Rent-Ready)

Unit has passed 100% of safety inspection, PM completed, fluids topped, fueled, washed, attachments torqued, and ERP updated to "Ready." Required Data: Unit ID, Inspector Signature, Date/Time Certified, Hour Meter.

Manager's Tips & Floor Controls
  • The 4-Hour Check-In Rule: If a delivery driver drops a machine at 2:00 PM, the return inspection, fluid check, and 4-point photos must be completed and logged into the ERP before 6:00 PM that same day.
  • Wash Rack Prioritization: Wash quick-turn, high-demand machines first. Never wash a unit that is headed for a 40-hour engine overhaul before washing 3 excavators that only need a 250-hour PM and a wash to hit the ready-line.
  • The Green Line Boundary: Green-tagged machines are parked strictly in designated "Ready-Line" bays facing forward with keys in the lockbox. A red-tagged or yellow-tagged unit is never permitted to cross into the ready-line staging area.
Rhythm & Operating Cadence
  • Daily 7:00 AM Yard Sweep: Shop Foreman and Lead Yard Specialist walk the return yard to verify all machines returned overnight have yellow tags and work orders opened.
  • Daily 1:00 PM Turnaround Handoff: Service Manager and Rental Counter Supervisor review the ready-line queue for afternoon customer dispatch.
  • Weekly Turnaround SLA Review: Every Tuesday at 8:00 AM, review average elapsed turnaround hours per asset class vs. the 24–48 hour SLA.
Live Fix / Hands-On Exercise
  1. Pull your branch's ERP Off-Rent Log for the trailing 14 days.
  2. Select 5 recent off-rent machine returns (e.g., 2 excavators, 2 aerial lifts, 1 wheel loader).
  3. Extract the exact timestamps: Physical Return Time, Work Order Open Time, Work Order Close Time, Ready-Line Release Time.
  4. Calculate the total turnaround hours for each unit.
  5. Identify the bottleneck stage responsible for >50% of the elapsed downtime for each machine.
  6. Re-engineer the staging flow to cut at least 24 hours of dwell time out of that stage.
Accountability Cascade
  • Return Gate & 4-Hour Check-In Owner: Lead Yard Specialist (Named individual).
  • Wash Rack Flow Owner: Wash Rack Lead / Yard Foreman.
  • Fast-Track PM & Repair Throughput Owner: Shop Foreman.
  • Final Ready-Line Sign-Off & ERP Status Owner: Rental Service Manager.
  • Escalation Trigger: Any machine sitting in yellow or red status for >48 hours without an active technician assigned must be escalated directly to the Branch Manager.

Module 2 — Fleet Economics
Module 2: Fleet Economics, Lifecycle Costs & OEC Management
Goal

Train managers to read equipment asset balance sheets, track repair spend against Original Equipment Cost (OEC), and evaluate when older units cross the economic tipping point into chronic profit drains.

Floor Rule
Any machine whose trailing 12-month maintenance cost exceeds 6% of OEC requires mandatory GM and Fleet Manager review before another dollar of labor or parts is authorized.
Part 1: Tracking Maintenance Cost as a % of OEC
What It Measures

Evaluates total annual maintenance expense (internal labor, replacement parts, lubricants, outside sublet repairs, and towing) relative to the original capital acquisition cost of the machine.

What It Does NOT Measure

Short-term seasonal rental demand or current market trade-in value.

Manager's Insight

Tracking maintenance as a raw dollar figure is misleading. Spending $10,000 on a $400,000 production excavator is exceptional (2.5% of OEC). Spending $10,000 on a $60,000 skid steer is a financial disaster (16.7% of OEC). OEC provides the normalized financial baseline across all asset categories. It tells you whether an asset is a cash generator or a cash incinerator.

Industry Standard Worked Example
MAINTENANCE COST AS % OF OEC FORMULA
Annual Maint % of OEC = Trailing 12-Mo Total Maint Spend / Original Equipment Cost × 100
  1. Step 1: Enter total internal labor loaded cost charged to the unit over the last 12 months. [YOUR NUMBER HERE]
  2. Step 2: Enter total parts and filter costs charged to the unit. [YOUR NUMBER HERE]
  3. Step 3: Enter all sublet repair, glass, tire, and outside vendor costs. [YOUR NUMBER HERE]
  4. Step 4: Sum parts, labor, and sublet to find Total 12-Month Maintenance Spend.
  5. Step 5: Divide by the machine's capitalized OEC. [YOUR NUMBER HERE]
  6. Step 6: Multiply by 100 to get the annual maintenance percentage.

Worked Examples (Industry Standard Benchmarks):

Machine A (20-Ton Excavator): OEC: $240,000. 12-Mo Maint Spend: $10,800. Calculation: ($10,800 / $240,000) × 100 = 4.5% → PASS (Within 3.5%–5.5% Target Band)

Machine B (Compact Track Loader - 5 Years Old): OEC: $68,000. 12-Mo Maint Spend: $7,480. Calculation: ($7,480 / $68,000) × 100 = 11.0% → CRITICAL FAIL (Disposition Alert)

HEDS Target Benchmark:
  • Target Band: 3.5% to 5.5% of OEC / Year
  • Warning Band: 5.6% to 6.5%
  • Critical Fail / Disposition Flag: > 6.5% of OEC
Part 2: Preventive Maintenance (PM) Compounding & ROI

The long-term return on investment of completing scheduled preventive maintenance on-time versus the compounding cost of jobsite breakdowns, towing, emergency repairs, and catastrophic engine/hydraulic failures.

The $450 vs. $12,000 Financial Contrast:

  • A routine 500-hour PM costs $450 (filters, oil, 2 hours labor) and takes 3 hours of planned shop time.
  • Skipping or deferring that PM causes contaminated hydraulic fluid or oil breakdown, resulting in a $12,000 catastrophic component rebuild, $1,200 in round-trip lowboy towing, $2,500 in customer machine substitution costs, and 20 days of lost rental revenue ($15,000). Total unbudgeted failure cost = $30,700.
  • PM ROI Ratio: Every $1.00 spent on disciplined, on-time PM prevents $6.00 to $10.00 in reactive failure and lost rental revenue.
PM COMPLIANCE RATE FORMULA
PM Compliance Rate % = PMs Completed Within ±10% or 50 Hours of Due Meter / Total Scheduled PMs Due × 100

HEDS Target Benchmark: ≥ 90% PM Compliance Rate (Top Quartile: ≥ 95%)

Part 3: The Economic Obsolescence Curve & 5% At-Risk Fleet Review

Identifies the bottom 5% of fleet assets that generate disproportionate maintenance drag, chronic telematics faults, and repeat downtime.

The Economic Obsolescence Curve:

  • Years 1–2 (0–2,000 hrs): Maintenance cost is low (2.0%–3.5% of OEC), covered by OEM warranty. High utilization.
  • Years 3–4 (2,000–5,000 hrs): Maintenance stabilizes in normal band (3.5%–5.0% of OEC). Predictable PM cycles.
  • Years 5+ (5,000+ hrs): The Tipping Point. Cumulative wear on pins, bushings, hydraulic pumps, engines, and emissions systems drives maintenance above 6.5% of OEC while utilization and rental rates decline.
  • Cumulative Lifetime Cap: When total cumulative lifetime maintenance spend reaches 35% to 40% of OEC, the asset has reached the end of its economic rental life.
Manager's Tips on Fleet Disposition
  • Package for Auction Before Major Failure: Do not spend $8,000 rebuilding a hydraulic pump on a 6,000-hour machine only to auction it 6 months later. Cut the asset loose before the major component overhaul.
  • Seasonal Timing: Present disposition recommendations to your GM in Q3/Q4 so equipment can hit spring contractor auctions at peak valuation.
Deliverable: 5% At-Risk Fleet Review Sheet

Complete audit of branch assets exceeding 6.0% Maint/OEC with clear repair-vs-auction recommendations for executive leadership.

Module 3 — Shop Workflow & WIP
Module 3: Shop Workflow, WIP Discipline & Labor Efficiency
Goal

Establish operational rigor on the shop floor to drive down cycle times, eliminate bay stagnation, and recover revenue faster.

Floor Rule
Fast-track jobs (PMs and turns <3 hours) never wait behind a 40-hour engine overhaul. Stage separate lanes so easy revenue returns to the yard immediately.
Part 1: Fast-Track Lanes & Triage Sequencing
What It Measures

The separation of high-velocity, low-hour maintenance tasks from long-dwell major repairs to prevent shop gridlock.

What It Does NOT Measure

Total fleet inventory value or branch overhead allocation.

Manager's Insight

If your shop only has one queue, small jobs get stuck behind big jobs. A 50-ton excavator waiting on a cylinder overhaul will tie up a bay for two weeks. If you pull a skid steer that needs a 30-minute hydraulic hose replacement into the back of that same bay, that skid steer sits down for two weeks. Fast-track lanes ensure quick revenue returns to the ready-line in hours, not weeks.

Standard Shop Lane Architecture:

  1. Lane 1: Fast-Track / Make-Ready: Dedicated bays for return safety inspections, fluid top-offs, minor wear items, and 250h PMs. Target completion: < 4 hours.
  2. Lane 2: Scheduled Mid-Level PMs: Dedicated bays for 500h/1000h scheduled services, hydraulic hose replacement, and track/tire adjustments. Target completion: < 24 hours.
  3. Lane 3: Major Repair & Component Overhaul: Dedicated bays for major engine, transmission, cylinder packings, and structural welding. Managed with strict milestone gates and parts staging.

MEAN TIME TO REPAIR (MTTR)
MTTR (Hours) = Total Hands-On Mechanical Repair Hours / Total Completed Repair Jobs

Part 2: Technician Productivity & Applied Wrench-Time
What It Measures

The percentage of paid technician payroll hours actively applied to open fleet maintenance and repair work orders.

What It Does NOT Measure

Invoice billing rates, customer revenue per hour, or departmental gross profit margin.

TECHNICIAN PRODUCTIVITY FORMULA
Technician Productivity % = Total Hours Logged on Fleet Work Orders / Total Paid Clocked Payroll Hours × 100

Worked Example: Shop has 5 technicians working 40 hours = 200 Total Paid Payroll Hours. Total hours booked to open fleet work orders = 174 hours. Unapplied hours = 26 hours. Calculation: (174 / 200) × 100 = 87.0% (PASS — Above 85% Target)

HEDS Target Benchmark: ≥ 85% Technician Productivity

Manager's Tips on Eliminating Lost Wrench-Time
  • Stop Using Techs as Yard Hostlers: Hire a lower-cost yard porter or wash-rack attendant to move machines in and out of bays. Paying a $38/hr master technician to jump-start and jockey machines across the yard is pure operational waste.
  • Pre-Stage Parts at the Bay: The technician should never walk to the parts room to look up filters. The parts expediter stages the PM kit in a tote at the bay before the machine is pulled in.
Part 3: Work-in-Process (WIP) Aging & The 5-Day WIP Scrub

Total labor, parts, and capital trapped in open, unclosed fleet work orders.

Why Fleet WIP Stagnates (The 4 Root Causes):

  1. Category A (Dealer Parts Delay): Tech disassembled machine, found broken part, ordered from dealer; machine sits in bay.
  2. Category B (Sublet Vendor Lag): Waiting on outside glass, hydraulic cylinder re-chroming, or track pressing vendor.
  3. Category C (Technician Reassigned): Job was paused to put tech on an emergency breakdown; work half-done.
  4. Category D (Administrative Ghost WIP): Repair was physically finished 3 days ago and machine is in the yard, but work order was never closed in the ERP.
HEDS Target Benchmark
Maximum internal fleet WIP aging: < 5 Business Days (excluding approved major overhauls).
Deliverable: Cleaned Fleet WIP Aging Board

Live audit of all open work orders in your ERP. Flag every job open >5 days. Categorize each into A, B, C, or D. Clear all Category D ghost tickets immediately. For Category A and B, establish an expedited vendor contact plan.

Module 4 — Regulatory & Safety
Module 4: Regulatory Compliance, Safety & Environmental Control
Goal

Install bulletproof compliance across shop facilities, mobile service trucks, and environmental waste management to eliminate legal liability, avoid regulatory fines, and protect technician safety.

Floor Rule
Compliance is an operational discipline, not an annual inspection. A service truck without current DOT medical/DVIR or a disabled bay without LOTO will shut down your shop faster than a broken machine.
Part 1: Facility & Shop Floor Safety (OSHA & ANSI)
What It Measures

Shop floor adherence to federal and industry life-safety standards, lifting equipment integrity, and energy isolation.

What It Does NOT Measure

Production throughput, technician efficiency, or financial performance metrics.

Core Facility Standards:

  • Lockout / Tagout (LOTO) Discipline: Mandatory physical lockout tags and keyed padlocks on all disabled equipment undergoing electrical, hydraulic, or powertrain repair.
  • Overhead Crane & Rigging Certifications: Annual load testing and daily visual inspection of wire rope, synthetic slings, shackles, and hoist limit switches.
  • Jack Stand & Heavy Blocking Standards: Prohibit working under machines supported solely by hydraulics. Mandatory certified steel blocking and rated jack stands.
  • Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) Compliance: 100% compliance on steel-toe boots, ANSI Z87.1 safety glasses, hearing protection in wash bays, and nitrile gloves for fluid handling.
Part 2: Mobile Service Fleet Compliance (DOT & Roadway)
What It Measures

Roadworthiness, regulatory compliance, and operator safety for all mobile field service trucks.

What It Does NOT Measure

Shop-based repair throughput or internal bay productivity.

Core Mobile Standards:

  • Driver Vehicle Inspection Reports (DVIR): Mandatory daily pre-trip and post-trip digital DVIR submissions before service trucks roll.
  • Gross Vehicle Weight Rating (GVWR) Management: Strict quarterly scale audits to ensure cranes, toolboxes, fluid tanks, and compressors do not exceed chassis GVWR.
  • Hours of Service (HOS) & Medical Cards: Compliance tracking for commercial driver licenses (CDL) and DOT medical certification for all field technicians.
  • Mobile Boom & Crane Certifications: Annual ANSI/OSHA certified dielectric and structural crane testing for all mobile service body cranes.
Part 3: Environmental Fluid Handling & Wash Bay Control (EPA)
What It Measures

Strict containment, storage, and disposal of hazardous waste fluids, hydrocarbons, and contaminated wash water.

What It Does NOT Measure

Production efficiency or cost per repair order. Strictly measures environmental regulatory adherence.

Core Environmental Standards:

  • Oil/Water Separator Maintenance: Monthly visual sediment checks and quarterly sludge extraction logs to prevent municipal sewer discharges.
  • Secondary Containment: 110% capacity secondary containment basins on all bulk new oil, waste oil, DEF, and antifreeze storage tanks.
  • Spill Kit Staging: Hydrocarbon spill kits staged at every wash rack, fluid transfer station, and inside every field service truck.
  • Used Filter Crushing & Disposal: Hot-draining and mechanical crushing of all used oil filters prior to certified recycling pickup.
HEDS Floor Rule
A technician removing 4 used oil filters during a 1,000-hour PM must hot-drain them for 12–24 hours, mechanically crush them to recover residual oil, and store them in a designated recycling drum. Throwing used filters into the municipal trash is an EPA violation.

HEDS Target Benchmarks:
  • Branch Compliance Audit Score: ≥ 95% on all 20-point facility & mobile audits
  • LOTO Compliance Rate: 100% on all repair orders involving electrical, hydraulic, or powertrain energy isolation

Module 5 — Lean Parts & Supply
Module 5: Lean Parts Management & Dealer Supply Integration
Goal

Master a lean, run-rate-based inventory model that guarantees 95%+ PM off-the-shelf fill rate without tying up excess capital in heavy dealership-level slow-moving inventory.

Floor Rule
Do not carry dealership-level inventory in a rental shop. Maintain a strict 90-day run-rate buffer of PM filters, fluids, teeth, and high-wear hoses. Everything else is procured on demand through structured dealer SLAs.
Part 1: The 90-Day (3-Month) Run-Rate Model
What It Measures

The alignment between on-hand maintenance stock and actual trailing consumption rates across the active fleet.

What It Does NOT Measure

OEM dealer floor plan inventory or wholesale parts distribution margins.

Manager's Insight

A dealership parts department carries $1.5M in inventory because they serve thousands of unknown retail customers who might walk in needing an obscure gear. A rental shop serves a fixed, known fleet of assets. You know exactly how many Tier 4 excavators and skid steers you own, and you know their exact service intervals. Carrying slow-moving hard parts on your shelves is dead working capital. Maintain a lean 90-day buffer of high-velocity wear and PM items, and lean on dealer supply chains for everything else.

Industry Standard Worked Example
90-DAY RUN-RATE MODEL FORMULAS
Monthly Run Rate = Trailing 6-Mo Parts Consumption / 6
90-Day Target Inventory = Monthly Run Rate × 3
Days of Supply = Current Inventory $ / Average Daily Parts Usage $
  1. Step 1: Enter trailing 6-month parts consumption for the SKU. [YOUR NUMBER HERE]
  2. Step 2: Divide by 6 to get Monthly Run Rate.
  3. Step 3: Multiply by 3 to get the 90-Day Target Stock Level.
  4. Step 4: Set Reorder Point (Min) = 1 month of supply. Maximum Stock Level (Max) = 3 months of supply.

Worked Example (Filter Inventory Run Rate): Part: Engine Oil Filter (P/N HF6510) used across 25 skid steers. Trailing 6-Month Consumption: 48 filters. Monthly Run Rate: 48 / 6 = 8 filters/month. Target 90-Day Stock Level: 8 × 3 = 24 filters. Reorder Point (Min): 8 filters (30-day buffer). Maximum Stock Level (Max): 24 filters (90-day buffer).

HEDS Target Benchmarks:
  • Run-Rate Stocking Window: Strict 90-Day (3-Month) Buffer on high-velocity PM and wear items.
  • Maintenance Parts Fill Rate: ≥ 95% for all scheduled PM kits.
  • Dead Stock / Obsolescence: < 3% of total inventory value with zero movement in >180 days (< 5% over 365 days).

Module 6 — Economic Downtime
Module 6: Economic Downtime, Telematics & Commercial Sync
Goal

Eliminate operational silos between the shop floor and the rental counter to eradicate turned-away business and maximize revenue recovery.

Floor Rule
When 5 machines are down and 2 techs are available, you do not fix the machine that is easiest. You fix the machine that has the highest daily rental rate and an active customer reservation.
Part 1: Revenue-Driven Repair Prioritization
What It Measures

Aligning shop bay capacity with commercial booking demand and daily earning potential.

What It Does NOT Measure

Seniority of the technician or chronological order of when machines arrived in the yard.

Manager's Insight

Technicians naturally gravitate toward the easiest or cleanest repair first. If a technician spends the morning fixing a $60/day light tower because it only needs a fuel filter, while a $850/day production excavator sits outside waiting on a 2-hour hose replacement, the branch loses $790/day in net earning capacity. The shop queue must be mathematically prioritized by economic impact.

Industry Standard Worked Example
SHOP PRIORITY INDEX (SPI)
SPI = Daily Book Rental Rate ($) / Estimated Mechanical Repair Hours
  1. Step 1: Enter the Daily Book Rental Rate for each down unit. [YOUR NUMBER HERE]
  2. Step 2: Enter the estimated repair hours for each unit. [YOUR NUMBER HERE]
  3. Step 3: Divide Daily Rate by Repair Hours to calculate SPI for each unit.
  4. Step 4: Rank units highest SPI to lowest. Assign your available technician to the highest SPI unit first.

Worked Scenario Walkthrough: 3 down machines, 1 available technician (8-hour shift):

  • Unit A: 50-Ton Excavator | Daily Rate: $850 | Repair: 2.0h | SPI = $850/2.0 = 425.0
  • Unit B: 8,000-lb Telehandler | Daily Rate: $240 | Repair: 1.5h | SPI = $240/1.5 = 160.0
  • Unit C: 19ft Scissor Lift | Daily Rate: $70 | Repair: 4.0h | SPI = $70/4.0 = 17.5

Decision Sequence: Unit A first (complete by 9:30 AM, unlocks $850/day), then Unit B (complete by 11:00 AM, unlocks $240/day). Combined revenue unlocked in 3.5 hours = $1,090/day. Unit C scheduled for the afternoon.

Module 7 — Operating Cadence
Module 7: Operating Cadence & HEDS KPI Management
Goal

Embed daily, weekly, and monthly management routines so the Service Manager runs the shop through data and visual controls rather than reactive firefighting.

Floor Rule
The 15-minute morning standup is non-negotiable. If you don't set the battle plan at 7:00 AM, the shop will run you instead of you running the shop.
Part 1: The 15-Minute Daily Rental Shop Standup
What It Measures

The discipline and consistency of daily shop floor communication: ready-line demand alignment, telematics alert triage, bay assignment throughput, and parts blocker resolution.

What It Does NOT Measure

Individual technician performance reviews, warranty claim processing, or customer billing accuracy. The standup is an operational alignment tool, not a performance review.

Manager's Insight

The standup is the single most important management tool in the rental shop. In 15 minutes, you align the shop's daily output with the rental counter's demand pipeline, triage telematics alerts before they become breakdowns, clear parts blockers before they stall bays, and lock in technician assignments with target out-times. Skip the standup once and you'll spend the rest of the day reacting to problems that 15 minutes of prevention would have eliminated.

Standup Protocol: Standing Up, No Chairs, Strict 15-Minute Timebox.

  • Time: 7:00 AM – 7:15 AM Sharp.
  • Attendees: Rental Service Manager, Shop Foreman, Lead Yard Specialist, Parts Expediter, Rental Counter Supervisor.
