Module 2: Service Financial Mechanics & Absorption Rate
Diagnose

Service departments frequently operate as standalone cost centers rather than branch absorption engines. When service absorption falls below 85%, branch overhead drains whole-goods margins during market downcycles.

Standard
  • Branch Service Absorption Target: Top-quartile target is 100% of total branch fixed overhead covered combined by Service + Parts gross profit. (Base target: 85%).
  • Service Labor Gross Margin Target: 68% minimum gross margin on total billable labor sales.
Industry Standard Worked Example
FORMULA & WORKED ABSORPTION CALCULATION
Branch Absorption % = [ (Service GP + Parts GP) / Total Branch Fixed Overhead ] × 100
  1. Service Gross Profit: Total Service Revenue ($250,000) minus Direct Tech COGS ($80,000) = $170,000 GP (68.0% Gross Margin).

    Service Gross Profit (GP) Explanation

    Service Gross Profit is the foundational metric for evaluating the financial health of a service department. It represents the revenue remaining after deducting the direct costs strictly associated with delivering that service (Cost of Goods Sold, or COGS).

    Using the baseline formula:

    • Total Service Revenue: $250,000
    • Less Direct Tech COGS: $80,000 (e.g., technician hourly wages, direct job materials)
    • Service Gross Profit: $170,000
    • Gross Margin: 68.0% ($170,000 ÷ $250,000)

    As a Service Manager, understanding the strict boundaries of this metric is critical for making accurate operational and pricing decisions.

    What Service Gross Profit Measures
    • Direct Labor Profitability: It reveals the direct return on investment for your technical workforce. A 68% margin indicates that for every dollar billed, 32 cents pays for the technician and direct materials, leaving 68 cents to fund the rest of the business.
    • Pricing Strategy Effectiveness: It validates whether your hourly rates, flat-rate pricing, or service contracts are set high enough to comfortably outpace the direct costs of fulfilling them.
    • Technician Efficiency and Utilization: Healthy margins typically indicate that technicians are highly billable and executing jobs efficiently. Shrinking margins often signal operational leaks like excessive unapplied time, unpaid callbacks (re-work), or unbilled overtime.
    • Job-Level Viability: It allows you to look at specific service lines (e.g., repairs vs. maintenance contracts) to see which types of jobs actually generate sustainable value for the company.
    What Service Gross Profit Does NOT Measure
    • Departmental Net Profit (The Bottom Line): A strong 68% gross margin can still result in a net loss if the business is overspending elsewhere. Gross profit is merely the starting point before operating expenses are deducted.
    • Overhead and Administrative Costs: It strictly excludes indirect costs. Your salary as a manager, dispatcher wages, CRM/software subscriptions, rent, vehicle depreciation, and office utilities are not factored into this number.
    • Marketing and Customer Acquisition Costs: It shows the profitability of the work itself, but ignores how much the company had to spend on advertising, lead generation, or sales commissions to acquire that $250,000 in revenue.
    • Cash Flow: Earning $170,000 in gross profit does not mean you have $170,000 in the bank. It does not account for unpaid invoices, aging accounts receivable, or debt servicing.
    Manager's Insight
    Think of Gross Profit as the "fuel" your technicians generate. It tells you exactly how much fuel you are pumping into the company engine, but it does not tell you how efficiently the rest of the company is burning that fuel to keep the lights on.
  2. Parts Gross Profit: Parts Department Monthly GP = $180,000 GP.

    Parts Gross Profit (GP) Explanation

    Parts Gross Profit is the core indicator of how effectively a parts department is buying and selling inventory. It represents the revenue remaining after deducting the actual acquisition cost of the parts sold (Cost of Goods Sold, or COGS).

    Because you only have the final GP figure, here is an illustrative formula to show how a department might arrive at that number:

    • Total Parts Revenue: $450,000 (Illustrative)
    • Less Parts COGS: $270,000 (The actual cost paid to the manufacturer/supplier for those parts)
    • Parts Gross Profit: $180,000
    • Gross Margin: 40.0% ($180,000 ÷ $450,000)

    As a Parts Manager, understanding what drives this number is essential for managing both pricing strategies and inventory acquisition.

    What Parts Gross Profit Measures
    • Pricing Matrix Effectiveness: It validates whether your retail, wholesale, and internal (service department) markups are generating a sufficient return. A blended margin of 40% usually indicates a healthy mix across these pricing tiers.
    • Purchasing Efficiency: It reflects the department's ability to buy at the right price. This includes maximizing manufacturer stock-order discounts, earning volume rebates, and avoiding margin-killing emergency freight costs.
    • Sales Channel Profitability: It allows you to analyze which revenue streams are most valuable. Retail and internal parts typically yield high margins, while wholesale operations generate lower margins but higher total volume.
    • Discounting Discipline: It highlights how well the counter staff is holding the line on pricing rather than giving away margin just to close a sale.
    What Parts Gross Profit Does NOT Measure
    • Departmental Net Profit: Earning $180,000 in gross profit is just the starting point. It completely excludes the payroll for parts managers, counter staff, delivery drivers, and warehouse workers.
    • Inventory Carrying Costs: GP strictly measures the parts you actually sold. It ignores the hidden, ongoing costs of holding inventory, such as warehouse square footage, insurance, shrinkage (theft/loss), and the cost of capital tied up in the bins.
    • Obsolescence Risk: A high gross profit this month does not reveal the financial drag of dead, non-moving inventory collecting dust in the back room. You can have strong margins on what you sell while simultaneously losing money on parts you can't sell.
    • Cash Flow: If your department does heavy wholesale business on net-30 or net-60 day terms, generating $180,000 in GP does not mean you collected that cash. It simply means the inventory left the building at a profitable markup.
    Manager's Insight
    Think of Parts Gross Profit as the spread between your buying power and your selling discipline. It tells you exactly how efficiently you are trading cash for inventory and back to cash—but it ignores the heavy overhead required to store, track, and deliver those parts.
  3. Total Aftermarket Gross Profit: $170,000 (Service) + $180,000 (Parts) = $350,000 Total GP.

    Total Aftermarket Gross Profit (GP) Explanation

    Total Aftermarket Gross Profit (also known as Fixed Operations Gross Profit) is the combined financial lifeblood of a dealership or service organization. It represents the total gross profit generated by the Parts and Service departments working in tandem to support customer equipment post-sale.

    Using your combined formula:

    • Service Gross Profit: $170,000
    • Parts Gross Profit: $180,000
    • Total Aftermarket GP: $350,000

    As a manager in the equipment or automotive industry, understanding this combined metric is crucial because it represents the foundational recurring revenue that sustains the business when front-end machine or vehicle sales slow down.

    What Total Aftermarket Gross Profit Measures
    • Fixed Absorption Capacity: This is the most critical use of this metric. Total Aftermarket GP is used to calculate the "Absorption Ratio"—measuring how much of the entire company's total fixed overhead (rent, utilities, admin salaries, etc.) can be paid strictly by the parts and service departments.
    • Departmental Synergy: It reflects how well Parts and Service are collaborating. Since the Service department is often the Parts department's largest customer, a healthy combined GP indicates that technicians are efficiently installing parts, and the parts department is sourcing them effectively to keep turnaround times low.
    • Customer Retention and Uptime: Consistently strong aftermarket GP is a direct indicator that customers are returning to you to maintain their assets. It shows you are successfully keeping their equipment running and minimizing their downtime.
    • True Service Capability: It provides a holistic view of post-sale operations. Evaluating Parts or Service in a vacuum can be misleading; combining them shows the total value generated by your technical and logistical expertise.
    What Total Aftermarket Gross Profit Does NOT Measure
    • Whole Goods / Equipment Sales Profit: This metric strictly excludes any revenue or profit generated by the front-end Sales department. It tells you nothing about the margins on new or used equipment sales.
    • Departmental Friction: A combined $350,000 GP looks great on paper, but it doesn't reveal if the departments are fighting over margin. For example, if Parts marks up internal sales too high, it might boost Parts GP while inflating the customer's repair bill, potentially hurting the Service department's ability to close the job.
    • Total Operating Net Profit: Just like the individual departmental GPs, this combined number has not yet paid for overhead, management salaries, marketing, or facility maintenance.
    • Inventory and Capital Efficiency: It shows the profit from the parts sold and labor billed, but completely ignores the massive capital tied up in the parts warehouse or the cost of purchasing specialized diagnostic tools and service trucks.
    Manager's Insight
    Think of Total Aftermarket Gross Profit as the financial anchor of the dealership. While the Sales department provides the spikes in revenue by selling the machines, the Aftermarket GP provides the steady, predictable cash flow required to pay the bills and keep the lights on during tough economic cycles.
  4. Total Branch Fixed Overhead: Rent, Admin, Sales Allocation, IT, Utilities = $330,000 Overhead.

    Total Branch Fixed Overhead Explanation

    Total Branch Fixed Overhead represents the mandatory, recurring operational expenses required to keep the branch's doors open, regardless of how much equipment is sold, how many parts are moved, or how many hours are billed. These are the indirect costs that must be paid just to exist.

    Based on your provided figures:

    • Rent / Facility (The physical building and grounds)
    • Administrative Costs (Salaries for GM, HR, accounting, and front desk)
    • Sales Allocation (Portion of shared corporate/facility expenses assigned to branch)
    • IT & Technology (Network infrastructure, dealer management systems, CRM subscriptions)
    • Utilities (Electricity, water, internet)
    • Total Branch Fixed Overhead: $330,000

    As a manager in a dealership environment, understanding fixed overhead is critical because this is the financial "burden" your parts and service gross profit is tasked with carrying.

    What Total Branch Fixed Overhead Measures
    • The "Keep the Lights On" Threshold: Establishes the absolute minimum gross profit the branch must generate each month before producing a single dollar of net profit.
    • The Target for Fixed Absorption: This $330,000 figure is the denominator in the critical Absorption Ratio. Because your Total Aftermarket GP is $350,000, your branch has an exceptional 106% Absorption Rate ($350,000 ÷ $330,000)—meaning Parts and Service pay 100% of the branch's bills plus generate a surplus.
    • Administrative & Facility Bloat: Tracking month-over-month highlights if indirect costs are spiraling (software costs, excessive admin hiring, utility inefficiency).
    • Shared Resource Costs: Shows the true cost of infrastructure supporting all departments—from the showroom floor to the service bays.
    What Total Branch Fixed Overhead Does NOT Measure
    • Cost of Goods Sold (COGS): Strictly excludes direct fulfillment costs. Technician hourly wages, direct parts purchases, and freight are deducted earlier to find Gross Profit.
    • Variable Expenses: Does not account for costs that fluctuate directly with sales volume (sales commissions, credit card fees, variable marketing).
    • Departmental Inefficiencies: High overhead is a facility/corporate issue. It does not mean technicians are slow or parts counter is mispricing; it means the building or management structure is too expensive.
    • Capital Expenditures: Does not measure purchasing a new service truck or building. Those are capitalized assets (though depreciation may be included in overhead).
    Manager's Insight
    When you compare your Total Aftermarket GP ($350,000) against this Fixed Overhead ($330,000), you cross the ultimate finish line of dealership management: Over-Absorption. Because your fixed operations cover all $330,000 of the branch overhead, every single piece of whole goods or equipment the sales department sells this month drops straight to the bottom line as pure net profit.
  5. Absorption Calculation: ($350,000 / $330,000) × 100 = 106.1% Absorption Rate.

    Absorption Rate Explanation

    Absorption Rate is widely considered the ultimate metric of financial health and stability for a dealership or equipment branch. It measures the percentage of the entire facility's fixed operating expenses that are completely covered by the gross profit generated solely from the parts and service departments (the aftermarket).

    Using the cumulative figures from the previous modules:

    • Total Aftermarket GP (The Engine): $350,000
    • Total Branch Fixed Overhead (The Burden): $330,000
    • Absorption Rate: 106.1% ($350,000 ÷ $330,000 × 100)

    As a Service Manager, understanding this metric elevates your perspective from running a single department to understanding your vital role in the branch's overall survival and profitability.

    What the Absorption Rate Measures
    • Financial Resilience (Bulletproofing): An absorption rate over 100% means the dealership is financially self-sufficient without having to sell a single piece of new or used equipment. If the economy crashes and sales drop to zero, the branch can still pay rent, keep the lights on, and make payroll purely on aftermarket operations.
    • Sales Profit Velocity: Because fixed operations cover 106.1% of overhead, financial pressure on Sales is eliminated. Every dollar of gross profit generated from selling whole goods drops directly to the bottom line as pure net profit (minus variable sales commissions).
    • True Fixed Operations Performance: Proves the combined strength of Parts and Service. Hitting 100%+ is the benchmark of a world-class aftermarket team.
    • Business Valuation: Dealerships with an absorption rate consistently over 100% are highly valued in the industry because they represent low-risk, cash-flowing businesses capable of surviving market downturns.
    What the Absorption Rate Does NOT Measure
    • Front-End Sales Performance: Tells you absolutely nothing about how many machines the dealership is selling, market share, or whole goods sales margins.
    • Internal Departmental Equity: A combined 106.1% rate is fantastic, but does not reveal who is doing the heavy lifting. Service could be at peak efficiency while Parts is struggling, or vice versa. Individual departmental GPs must still be managed.
    • Variable Expenses: Strictly measures against fixed overhead. Does not account for variable costs like sales commissions, bonuses, or marketing budgets tied to specific promotions.
    • Total Branch Net Profit: While >100% absorption guarantees aftermarket profitability after overhead, final branch net profit depends on whether Sales generates positive margins or loses money on bad trades/heavy discounting.
    Manager's Insight
    An absorption rate of 106.1% is the "Holy Grail" of dealership management. When you walk into branch management meetings with this number, you have effectively paid the mortgage, the electric bill, and the GM's salary with your technicians' wrenches and your parts counter's invoices. You have transformed the aftermarket from a support function into the financial anchor of the entire business.
  6. Result: TOP QUARTILE PASS — Branch covers 100%+ of fixed overhead from aftermarket operations.
HEDS Floor Rule
Never discount labor rates to win a job. Every dollar of discounted labor comes 100% out of bottom-line net profit, not overhead.

Module 3: Labor & Payroll Reconciliation (The Unbilled Labor Gap)
Exercise Overview

Audit and calculate the exact dollar leakage between technician paid payroll hours and billed/WIP hours for the prior week.

Industry Standard Worked Example (10 Technician Shop)
CALCULATION WALKTHROUGH
  1. Step 1 (Total Paid Payroll): 10 Techs @ 40 hrs/wk = 400 Clocked Payroll Hours.
    What it Measures

    This is your total labor inventory for the week. It represents the absolute maximum capacity your shop has available to sell. You are paying the technicians for 400 hours of their time, regardless of what they do while on the clock.

    What it Does NOT Measure

    It tells you absolutely nothing about productivity, efficiency, or how much revenue was generated. It is strictly a measure of time purchased by the dealership.

    Manager's Insight
    Treat these 400 hours exactly like a fresh shipment of 400 expensive parts with an expiration date of Friday at 5:00 PM. If you do not sell them by the time the shop closes, they go in the dumpster. You can never get them back.
  2. Step 2 (Closed RO Billed Hours): Total hours billed on invoiced Work Orders = 290 Billed Hours.
    What it Measures

    This is the actual labor sold and finalized during the week. These are the hours that directly generate cash, hit the income statement, and contribute to your Service Gross Profit and branch absorption.

    What it Does NOT Measure

    It does not represent all the work your technicians performed this week. Because larger diagnostic or teardown jobs often span multiple weeks, this number excludes active work performed on machines still sitting in the bays.

    Manager's Insight
    This is the only number that actually pays the bills. A technician sweating for 8 straight hours doesn't matter to the financial statement unless those hours land here on a closed, paid invoice. Your primary daily objective is moving hours from the shop floor onto closed tickets.
  3. Step 3 (Open WIP Added Hours): Total hours logged to active open Work Orders = 70 WIP Hours.
    What it Measures

    Work In Progress (WIP) measures the labor performed this week on jobs that are not yet finished or invoiced (e.g., a transmission rebuild waiting on a sub-assembly). This is "trapped" revenue that you have earned but cannot yet collect.

    What it Does NOT Measure

    WIP is not cash flow, and it does not count toward this week's gross profit. You cannot deposit WIP in the bank.

    Manager's Insight
    WIP is a necessary evil for major repairs, but it is also the easiest hiding place for stalled, problematic jobs. As a manager, you must aggressively police WIP to ensure jobs are waiting on parts, not waiting on someone to make a decision or call the customer for authorization.
  4. Step 4 (Unapplied Lost Gap): 400 - (290 + 70) = 40 Unapplied Lost Hours / Week (10% unapplied leakage).
    What it Measures

    This is the "black hole" of technician time. It represents the hours you paid for but cannot bill to a customer or attribute to an ongoing job. This time is typically lost to sweeping floors, waiting in line at the parts counter, waiting for dispatch, or undocumented diagnostic rework.

    What it Does NOT Measure

    It identifies the size of the leak, but it does not tell you the root cause. A high unapplied gap is a symptom of poor shop processes, but the manager must physically investigate the floor to find out why technicians aren't turning wrenches.

    Manager's Insight
    This is where your profit margin bleeds out. A 10% leakage rate might sound "acceptable" on paper, but if you ran a parts department and 10% of your inventory fell off the delivery truck every single week, you would be fired. Find the leak and plug it.
  5. Step 5 (Weekly Cash Revenue Loss): 40 hrs × $185 Posted Matrix Rate = $7,400 Direct Weekly Loss.
    What it Measures

    This assigns a tangible financial cost to your shop's inefficiency. It calculates the opportunity cost—the exact amount of top-line revenue the dealership missed out on because those 40 hours were wasted instead of being billed at the shop's standard retail labor rate.

    What it Does NOT Measure

    This is not cash physically stolen from the register, nor is it a hard expense on the profit and loss statement. It represents invisible lost potential. It also doesn't account for the lost parts sales that typically accompany every billed labor hour.

    Manager's Insight
    Translating lost hours into lost dollars is how you get the dealer principal's attention. Telling upper management you have "40 unapplied hours" sounds like a minor scheduling quirk; telling them you lost "$7,400 in billable revenue this week" gets you the budget approval to fix the problem.
  6. Step 6 (Annualized Impact): $7,400 × 52 weeks = $384,800 Annual Profit Leakage.
    What it Measures

    The long-term severity of operational leaks. It demonstrates to a manager how a seemingly minor daily inefficiency compounds into a massive hit to the dealership's yearly bottom line and fixed absorption rate.

    What it Does NOT Measure

    It does not mean you can easily recover all $384,800. A shop will never realistically hit 0% unapplied time—technicians must attend mandatory safety meetings, perform bay maintenance, and complete factory training.

    Manager's Insight
    When you are fighting for a $15,000 special tooling budget or requesting approval to hire a $40,000-a-year parts runner to keep your technicians in their bays, this $384,800 is your primary ammunition. You spend a little overhead intentionally to capture a massive amount of lost gross profit.

Module 4: Work-in-Progress (WIP) Aging & Cash Flow Control
Diagnose & Standard

Open Work-in-Progress (WIP) represents unbilled labor and installed parts paid for by the dealership. Aging WIP drains operating cash flow and hides job overruns.

  • Total Open WIP Limit: Maximum 10 days of average daily service revenue.
  • Aged WIP (>30 Days): $0 tolerance without written Branch Manager authorization.
Industry Standard Worked Example
FORMULA & WORKED CALCULATION
Days of Open WIP = Total Unbilled WIP Dollars / Average Daily Service Revenue
  1. Total Open WIP Ledger Balance: Unbilled Labor + Parts + Sublet = $105,000 WIP Balance.
    What it Measures

    The sum total of all unbilled technician labor, installed parts, and outside sublet work logged onto open Work Orders currently active in the dealership system.

    What it Does NOT Measure

    It does not represent cash in hand or earned gross profit. It is uncollected working capital tied up in iron on your lot or in your shop bays.

    Manager's Insight
    Think of open WIP as cash taken out of the dealer's bank account and physically laid on the shop floor. Every day a job sits unbilled, that cash gathers dust and risks becoming uncollectible due to dispute or missing purchase orders.
  2. Average Daily Service Revenue: $250,000 monthly revenue / 21 working days = $11,905 / Day.
    What it Measures

    The baseline daily financial burn rate and revenue generation speed of your service department ($250,000 monthly labor revenue ÷ 21 working days).

    What it Does NOT Measure

    Daily cash collections or daily gross profit dollars; it is strictly a revenue-per-operating-day benchmark.

    Manager's Insight
    This daily number is your operational yardstick. You must generate at least $11,905 in closed invoices every single working day just to maintain your baseline monthly financial plan.
  3. Days of Open WIP Calculation: $105,000 / $11,905 = 8.8 Days of WIP (PASS ≤ 10 DAYS).
    What it Measures

    The total length of time (in operating days) it takes for your service department to convert active work order inventory into closed, billable invoices ($105,000 WIP ÷ $11,905/day = 8.8 Days).

    What it Does NOT Measure

    It does not distinguish between active major rebuilds (which naturally take several days) and small 2-hour PM jobs that should be closed within 24 hours.

    Manager's Insight
    Top-quartile heavy equipment dealerships operate under 10 Days of WIP. Anything over 10 days signals severe administrative bottlenecks in job closing, customer authorization delays, or parts backorders.
  4. Aged WIP >30 Days Audit: Open ledger shows $14,500 sitting >30 days (FAIL (AGED RISK)).
    What it Measures

    The dollar amount of unbilled work order balance that has been sitting open in your ERP system for more than 30 calendar days without being invoiced.

    What it Does NOT Measure

    The technical complexity or quality of the repair work performed. A job sitting over 30 days is almost always an administrative or communication failure, not a technical one.

    Manager's Insight
    Aged WIP >30 days is a financial emergency. The likelihood of collecting 100% of an unbilled job drops by 50% once it crosses 30 days. Require a weekly Monday morning "WIP Scrub" with your advisors to force every aged ticket to closure, warranty submission, or write-off review.
HEDS Floor Rule
Work orders must be closed and invoiced within 24 hours of technician job completion. Touch it, finish it, bill it.

Module 5: Technician Efficiency, Productivity, & Utilization
Industry Standard Worked Example
BENCHMARK WORKED CALCULATION
  1. Shop Technicians: Billed 340 hrs / Clocked 380 hrs = 89.5% Shop Efficiency (PASS ≥ 85%).
    What it Measures

    The conversion rate of internal shop technician clocked hours into flat-rate billed hours (340 Billed Hrs ÷ 380 Clocked Hrs = 89.5%).

    What it Does NOT Measure

    Field service performance, travel time recovery, or the overall size of the shop's labor force. It strictly measures shop-bay wrench time efficiency against job standards.

    Manager's Insight
    Top-quartile shop efficiency is 85%+. When shop efficiency drops below 85%, the culprit is rarely slow technicians—it is usually waiting for parts, waiting for crane or wash bay availability, or poor diagnostic direction from supervisors.
  2. Field Technicians: Billed 150 hrs / Clocked 195 hrs (incl. drive time) = 76.9% Field Efficiency (PASS ≥ 75%).
    What it Measures

    Field service technician billable yield relative to total paid on-job hours, including job site travel, setup, and remote diagnostic time (150 Billed Hrs ÷ 195 Clocked Hrs = 76.9%).

    What it Does NOT Measure

    Field service truck fuel/maintenance overhead or customer mileage/zonal travel billings.

    Manager's Insight
    Field efficiency benchmark is 75%+ because travel time inherently dilutes billable hours. To protect field efficiency, ensure field trucks are pre-stocked with fast-moving PM parts and dispatch routes are optimized to avoid double-backing.
HEDS Floor Rule (2-Stage Parts Rule)
For PMs and planned jobs, parts are pre-pulled and staged at the bay prior to assignment. For teardown diagnostics, initial diagnostic parts pull occurs within 2 hours of teardown completion. Technicians never wait at parts counter lines.
Day 2 Slot 6 — Execution & Pricing
Module 6: Effective Labor Rate (ELR) & Discounting Control
Industry Standard Worked Example
ELR RECOVERY CALCULATION
  1. Total Billed Labor Revenue: Collected labor sales = $62,900.
    What it Measures

    The net dollar volume collected strictly from technician labor billing across customer, warranty, and internal work order segments.

    What it Does NOT Measure

    Parts revenue, environmental fees, shop supplies fees, or travel/mileage charges.

    Manager's Insight
    Labor revenue is pure service production output. If labor revenue drops while technician payroll stays constant, your labor gross margin immediately collapses.
  2. Total Billed Labor Hours: 370 Billed Hours.
    What it Measures

    The total count of flat-rate or time-and-material hours billed to customers on closed work order tickets.

    What it Does NOT Measure

    Technician clocked hours or unbilled diagnostic hours spent researching factory service bulletins.

    Manager's Insight
    Billed hours are the primary engine of dealer gross profit. Tracking billed hours daily—rather than waiting for end-of-month financial statements—allows you to correct weekly pacing before it's too late.
  3. Actual Realized ELR: $62,900 / 370 hrs = $170.00 / hr Actual Realized Rate.
    What it Measures

    The true, effective average dollar rate realized for every single labor hour billed ($62,900 Billed Labor Rev ÷ 370 Billed Hrs = $170.00/hr).

    What it Does NOT Measure

    The posted retail standard matrix rate on the wall, nor does it guarantee profit if technician wages are unaligned.

    Manager's Insight
    Realized ELR is the ultimate test of pricing discipline. If your posted rate is $185/hr but your realized ELR is $170/hr, you are losing $15 on every billed hour to unauthorized discounts, capped warranty rates, or unbilled diagnostic time.
  4. ELR Matrix Recovery %: ($170.00 / $185.00) × 100 = 91.9% Recovery (WARN (< 92% TARGET)).
    What it Measures

    The percentage of your posted retail standard matrix rate that actually makes it onto closed invoices ($170.00 Realized ÷ $185.00 Posted = 91.9%).

    What it Does NOT Measure

    Total shop billable volume; you can have 100% recovery on very low volume, which still results in a net branch loss.

    Manager's Insight
    HEDS target is ≥ 92% ELR recovery. A drop below 92% indicates advisors are overriding matrix rates, giving away diagnostic time, or failing to bill national account matrix pricing properly.
HEDS Floor Rule
Diagnostic time is billable time. No technician begins diagnostic work without a customer-signed authorization for an initial 2-hour diagnostic minimum.
Day 2 Slot 7 — Execution & Pricing
Module 7: Triage, Dispatch, & Segmented Scheduling
Industry Standard Worked Example
FIRST-TIME FIX & TRIAGE VELOCITY
  1. Total Completed ROs (Past 30 Days): 140 closed Work Orders.
    What it Measures

    Total work order volume closed and invoiced across the entire service department over a rolling 30-day period.

    What it Does NOT Measure

    Average job size, revenue per ticket, or repair complexity.

    Manager's Insight
    Understanding total ticket volume establishes your shop's throughput baseline. It helps calculate administrative workload per service advisor and parts counter representative.
  2. Repeat Return Jobs (Past 30 Days): 9 jobs returned for repeat complaint.
    What it Measures

    The count of customer machines returning to the shop within 30 days for the same uncorrected symptom or failure caused by improper initial repair.

    What it Does NOT Measure

    Unrelated new failures on the same machine or routine scheduled maintenance visits.

    Manager's Insight
    Repeat jobs are poison to service profitability. They create unpaid rework, tie up technician bays, destroy customer trust, and kill your labor gross margin because rework hours cannot be billed.
  3. First-Time Fix Rate (FTFR): [ 1 - (9 / 140) ] × 100 = 93.6% FTFR (PASS ≥ 88%).
    What it Measures

    The percentage of customer equipment repairs completed correctly on the very first attempt without requiring return work [(1 - (9 / 140)) × 100 = 93.6%].

    What it Does NOT Measure

    Diagnostic velocity or customer wait time prior to shop entry.

Day 3 Slot 11 — Management Systems
Module 11: The 10-Minute Morning Standup
Diagnose

Most service department meetings take 30 minutes and produce zero decisions. The standup fixes this by being short, structured, and mandatory. It is a daily meeting — same time, same agenda, same attendees — that lasts 10 minutes and produces one decision minimum.

Standard

The standup is the heartbeat of the shop. If it does not happen, the shop drifts. If it happens but produces no decisions, it is a waste of time.

Daily Rhythm
10-MINUTE STANDUP AGENDA
  1. How many technicians are available today? (2 minutes)
  2. What jobs are carryover from yesterday? (2 minutes)
  3. Are there parts roadblocks for today's scheduled work? (1 minute)
  4. Which quotes are over 48 hours old? (1 minute)
  5. Capacity stress flag: Does the 2-week forecast show scheduled hours exceeding available hours by more than 15%? If yes, what is the plan — throttle, overtime, or bring in field techs? (2 minutes)
  6. One decision: Who owns what by end of shift? (2 minutes)
Rules
  • 10 minutes. Not 15. Not 30. Ten.
  • Everyone stands. No sitting.
  • The manager asks the questions. The advisor and foreman answer.
  • One decision minimum. If no decision is needed, the standup is 3 minutes and everyone goes to work. The purpose is rhythm, not duration.
HEDS Floor Rule
If your standup takes 30 minutes, you are having a meeting, not a standup. Cut it to 10. If there is nothing to discuss, the standup is 3 minutes and everyone goes to work. The purpose is rhythm, not duration.

The 6-item agenda printed and posted in the shop. Timed. Signed by the manager. Practiced during the onsite with the cohort.

Day 4 Slot 13 — Process & People
Module 13: The Work Order Lifecycle
Diagnose

Jobs stall because steps are skipped. The work order lifecycle is not a suggestion — it is the production line. If a step is skipped, the job stalls and the cash stops. Treat the lifecycle like a factory floor, because that is what it is.

Process Standard
5-STEP WORK ORDER LIFECYCLE
  1. Step 1: Structured Intake (Before Work Begins)
    • Obtain clear complaint description from the customer (Four C's: Complaint, Cause, Correction, Coverage) — The 4-C standard applies to both retail customer intake and OEM warranty claims.
    • Record machine hours, serial number, and customer contact information
    • Get customer pre-authorization for labor before any work starts
    • No technician touches the machine until intake is complete and approved
    Floor Rule
    If the complaint is not clear, the diagnosis will be wrong. If the diagnosis is wrong, the quote is wrong. If the quote is wrong, the job stalls. Intake is where leaks start — and where they are cheapest to prevent.
  2. Step 2: Technician Dispatch
    • Match technician skill level to job complexity
    • Technician reviews complaint and machine history with advisor before starting
    • If complaint and history do not match, STOP. Call the customer. Do not guess.
  3. Step 3: Parts Staging
    • Parts pulled and staged before the technician begins disassembly
    • If parts are on backorder, the WO is not dispatched until parts arrive — or the customer authorizes partial repair
    • Parts person confirms staging to the advisor, not just to the parts shelf
    Floor Rule
    A technician standing at a disassembled machine waiting for parts is the most expensive person in your building. Stage parts before teardown or do not start the job.
  4. Step 4: Final Review and Rapid Invoicing
    • Technician submits time and 4-C documentation within 24 hours of mechanical completion. Advisor reviews and invoices within 48 hours of mechanical completion.
    • The 48-hour clock starts when labor is complete — not when parts arrive
    • Advisor reviews final invoice with foreman before releasing to billing
    • Any write-down or discount requires manager signature on the invoice
  5. Step 5: Customer Pickup and Confirmation
    • Customer signs Four C's copy at pickup or receives digital confirmation
    • Customer receives explanation of work completed, parts replaced, and any remaining recommendations
    • Service advisor follows up within 7 days to confirm satisfaction
HEDS Floor Rule
The work order lifecycle is not a suggestion. It is the production line. If a step is skipped, the job stalls and the cash stops. Treat the lifecycle like a factory floor — because that is what it is.
Deliverable: Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) Map

One-page SOP for the complete work order lifecycle, customized to the student's shop. Identifies the three weakest links and the standards installed to fix them.

Module 16: Forecasting & Leading Indicators
Diagnose

By the time the accounting report catches a problem, the damage is done. Lagging indicators tell you what happened. Leading indicators tell you what is about to happen. A manager who only reads lagging indicators is driving by looking in the rearview mirror.

Concept Shift
LEADING vs. LAGGING INDICATORS
Lagging (What Happened) Leading (What Is About to Happened)
Monthly P&LOpen WO aging trend this week
Quarterly absorption reportQuote-to-close ratio this week
Annual revenue summaryTechnician capacity utilization forecast for next 2 weeks
Month-end unbilled labor $Parts backorder trend this week

The Shift: Stop asking "What did we do last month?" Start asking "What is about to break next week?"

Capacity Forecasting
2-WEEK CAPACITY STRESS FLAG

Look 2-3 weeks ahead. Compare available technician hours against scheduled and quoted hours. If scheduled exceeds available by more than 15%, you have a capacity stress flag.

FORMULA
Capacity Stress = (Scheduled + Quoted Hours for Next 2 Weeks ÷ Available Tech Hours for Next 2 Weeks) × 100

If Capacity Stress > 115%:

  • Throttle incoming work (do not accept rush jobs without manager approval)
  • Schedule overtime in advance, not at the last minute
  • Bring in field technicians or loaners from other branches
  • Communicate revised ETAs to customers before they call you
HEDS Floor Rule
Lagging indicators tell you what happened. Leading indicators tell you what is about to happen. A manager who only reads lagging indicators is driving by looking in the rearview mirror. The capacity stress flag from the 2-week forecast should be the first topic of discussion at every Tuesday morning standup.
